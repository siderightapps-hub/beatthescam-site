## 1. What a fake Lloyds text looks like

A Lloyds scam text is a smishing (SMS phishing) message that pretends to be from the bank so you tap a link and give away your login details, card numbers, or a one-time passcode. A common version reads something like: 'LLOYDS: A new payee J SMITH was added to your account. If this wasn't you, cancel here: lloyds-verify-account.com'. The hook changes — a new payee, a blocked card, a payment 'on hold' — but the aim is always to make you panic and tap before you think. The link opens a copycat Lloyds login page that captures whatever you enter. This guide shows how to tell a real Lloyds message from a fake, what to do if you've already tapped one, and how to report it.


## 2. Why the fake can look so real

Scammers can set the sender name to display as 'Lloyds', so a fake message can land in the same conversation thread as the genuine alerts you've had before. That means you can't trust a text by its sender name or by where it sits in your phone. Criminals send the same message to huge lists of UK numbers at once, knowing a share of recipients will bank with Lloyds. The text borrows Lloyds branding, copies the tone of a real fraud alert, and adds a deadline so you rush. None of that makes it genuine: a real Lloyds alert will never need you to follow a link to log in, confirm a password or passcode, or move money to a 'safe account'.


## 3. Signs a Lloyds text is a scam

- It rushes you — a payee 'added', a card 'blocked', or access lost unless you respond by a deadline.
- It links you off to 'cancel', 'verify', or 'secure' your account, log in, or re-enter card details.
- The link goes to a lookalike or unusual domain. Banks may use more than one genuine owned domain, so do not judge by the address alone — the rule that always holds is never to use a text-message link to log in, verify details, enter a code, or move money. Open the Lloyds app or type the address yourself.
- It asks for your full password, memorable information, card number, or a one-time passcode — Lloyds will never ask you to share these.
- It tells you to move money to a 'new' or 'safe' account to protect it.
- A generic 'Dear Customer' greeting can be a clue, but it's weaker by text than email — genuine bank texts are often short — so weigh it with the other signs.
- Small errors can be clues — odd spacing, a foreign number, or wording that isn't quite official — but many scam texts are polished, so a tidy message proves nothing.


## 4. How the scam plays out, step by step

First, an unexpected text appears to come from Lloyds about a problem — a new payee, a blocked payment, or a login you don't recognise. Second, it pushes you to tap a link to 'cancel' or 'restore' access. Third, the link opens a fake Lloyds page that mirrors the real one and asks for your username, password and memorable information. Fourth, the moment you enter them the criminals have your details in real time. Fifth, they trigger a genuine Lloyds one-time passcode and the fake page asks you to type it in, which lets them approve a payment or add a payee. Finally, they move money out or apply for credit in your name. It can take minutes, so pausing at the first text is your strongest defence.


## 5. Check it safely, without tapping anything

Never use a number or link from the text itself — that's how scammers keep you in their script. The simplest check is to call 159: a secure service, recognised across the UK banking industry, that connects you safely and directly to your bank, and Lloyds takes part. You can also open the official Lloyds app, or type lloydsbank.com into your browser by hand, and review your recent activity and messages there. Or call Lloyds on the number printed on the back of your card. Not every genuine text shows up as an in-app message, so if you can't confirm what the text claims, contact Lloyds directly rather than assuming either way. If you're unsure whether a linked site is a copycat, our guide on /guides/is-this-website-a-scam/ runs through the checks.


## 6. If you tapped the link or shared details

Act quickly, but don't panic. If you entered your login or a passcode, call 159 straight away, or use the number on the back of your card, tell Lloyds you've been phished, and ask them to secure your account and check for payees or payments you didn't set up. Change your Lloyds password from inside the official app or by typing the address yourself, and change it anywhere you reused it. If you shared card details, ask the bank to stop and reissue the card. If you sent money, tell the bank at once — since 7 October 2024, most APP (authorised push-payment) fraud victims can claim reimbursement for UK transfers over Faster Payments or CHAPS, subject to limits and exclusions, and there's a deadline to claim, so report it as fraud promptly. To guard your identity, consider a Cifas Protective Registration at cifas.org.uk, and review your credit report with Experian, Equifax or TransUnion for anything you don't recognise. Keep the text as evidence.


## 7. How to report a Lloyds scam text (UK)

Forward the text to 7726 — it is free, and it reports the message to your mobile provider, which can investigate and take action. Report it to Report Fraud at reportfraud.police.uk or on 0300 123 2040 if you're in England, Wales or Northern Ireland; in Scotland, report to Police Scotland on 101. If a similar scam reached you by email, forward it to the National Cyber Security Centre at report@phishing.gov.uk. Tell Lloyds too, through the app or the number on your card, so they can warn other customers. Keep a screenshot and a note of what you did and when.


## FAQ

**Is a Lloyds text with a link always a scam?**

A link alone isn't proof either way. What matters is what the message asks you to do: never use a link in a text to log in, confirm details, enter a one-time code, or move money. If a message pushes you toward any of that, treat it as a scam and check independently — through the Lloyds app, the number on your card, or 159.

**Can a scam text appear in my real Lloyds message thread?**

Yes. Scammers can spoof the sender name so 'Lloyds' is shown, which can drop a fake into the same thread as your genuine alerts. Never trust a text just because of the sender name or where it sits — verify it independently before acting.

**What number should I call to check a Lloyds text safely?**

Call 159 — a secure service that connects you safely and directly to your bank, and Lloyds takes part — or use the number printed on the back of your card. Never call a number from the suspicious text itself.

**I tapped the link and entered my details — what now?**

Call 159 or the number on your card straight away and tell Lloyds you've been phished so they can secure your account. Change your password in the official app, ask them to stop your card if you shared card details, and report any payment you didn't make. Then report it to Report Fraud at reportfraud.police.uk and consider a Cifas Protective Registration at cifas.org.uk.

**Can I get my money back if I paid a Lloyds scammer?**

Often, yes. Tell your bank immediately. Since 7 October 2024, most victims of authorised push-payment fraud can claim reimbursement on UK transfers over Faster Payments or CHAPS, subject to limits, exclusions and a claiming deadline — so report it as fraud quickly and keep every message. If you paid by card, ask about a chargeback.
