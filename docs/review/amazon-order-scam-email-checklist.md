## 1. What a fake Amazon email looks like

An Amazon email scam is a phishing message that imitates Amazon so you click a link and hand over your login, card details, or a one-time code. A typical one comes from a display name like "Amazon" with a subject such as `Your order has been placed - action required` or `Your account has been locked`, and a button like `Verify your account`. The pretext changes — a £799 order you didn't place, a refund waiting, a payment that 'failed', a Prime renewal — but the goal is to rush you onto a fake Amazon sign-in page. The link often points to a lookalike such as `amazon-account-verify.example`. This guide shows how to tell a genuine Amazon email from a fake, how to check safely, and how to report it.


## 2. Why these Amazon emails fool people

Phishing emails copy Amazon's logo, colours and layout, and the visible sender name can be set to anything, so "Amazon" in the From line proves nothing. The link text can read like amazon.co.uk while pointing somewhere else, and the message may even be sent from a hacked mailbox. Scammers send millions at once, knowing most people have an Amazon account, and lean on urgency — a big order, a locked account, a deadline. None of that makes it real: a genuine Amazon email will not ask you to confirm your password, card number, or a one-time code by following a link, or to pay outside Amazon's own checkout.


## 3. Signs an Amazon email is phishing

- A generic greeting such as `Dear Customer` instead of your name — a stronger clue by email than by text.
- The sender's address isn't a genuine Amazon domain, or a link (hover over it without clicking) points to an unfamiliar site — but addresses and links can be disguised, so never use an email link to sign in.
- It pushes you to `verify`, `reactivate`, or `confirm` your account through a button or link.
- It asks for your password, full card number, bank details, or a one-time code — Amazon will not ask for these by email.
- It wants payment by gift card, bank transfer, or any method outside Amazon's normal checkout.
- An unexpected attachment, or an order confirmation for something you never bought (designed to make you click `cancel`).
- Spelling or layout that's slightly off — though many phishing emails are polished, so a tidy email proves nothing.


## 4. How the Amazon email scam works

First, an email lands that looks like an Amazon alert — an order, a refund, or a locked account. Second, it pushes you to click a button to `fix` or `verify`. Third, the link opens a near-perfect copy of the Amazon sign-in page. Fourth, anything you type — email, password, card details — is captured by the criminals in real time. Fifth, some pages then ask for a one-time code so they can get past two-step verification. Finally, they take over the account, place orders, harvest your saved cards, or use the details in further scams. Checking inside your real Amazon account, rather than through the email, breaks the chain before step four.


## 5. How to check if an Amazon email is genuine

Do not click links or open attachments in the email. Instead, check independently. Open the Amazon app or type amazon.co.uk into your browser yourself (or use a saved bookmark) and sign in, then look in `Your Orders` and the `Message Centre` under Your Account — genuine Amazon messages are shown there, and a real order will appear in your order history. If the email mentions an order you can't see, it's fake. Amazon does not handle payments or refunds by emailed link, so treat any such request as a scam. Hovering over a link can hint where it really goes, but it can be disguised, so don't rely on it alone. If you're unsure whether a linked site is a copycat, our guide on /guides/is-this-website-a-scam/ walks through the checks.


## 6. If you clicked or shared details

Act quickly but stay calm. If you entered your Amazon password, sign in via the app or amazon.co.uk and change it, turn on two-step verification, and check `Your Orders`, saved payment methods, and addresses for anything you didn't add. If you entered card details, contact your bank or card issuer using the number on your card, tell them it was a scam, and ask them to stop the card and dispute the payment (a card payment may be recoverable through chargeback). If you sent money by bank transfer, report it as fraud immediately — since 7 October 2024, most APP (authorised push-payment) fraud victims can claim reimbursement for UK transfers made over Faster Payments or CHAPS, subject to limits and exclusions — the PSR rules include a 13-month claim window, a maximum claim of £85,000, possible exclusions, and a possible excess of up to £100. Consider a Cifas Protective Registration at cifas.org.uk and review your credit report with Experian, Equifax or TransUnion. Amazon scammers also call people by phone — see our /guides/amazon-phone-call-scam-uk/ guide. Keep the email as evidence.


## 7. How to report a fake Amazon email (UK)

Forward the email to the National Cyber Security Centre at report@phishing.gov.uk, which can investigate and take down phishing sites. You can also report it to Amazon by forwarding it to stop-spoofing@amazon.com. If you lost money or shared sensitive details, report it to Report Fraud at reportfraud.police.uk or on 0300 123 2040 if you are in England, Wales or Northern Ireland; in Scotland, report to Police Scotland on 101. If the same scam reached you by text, forward that to 7726. Keep the email and a note of what you did, in case you need it later.


## FAQ

**Does Amazon send emails with links?**

Genuine Amazon emails can contain links, so a link alone isn't proof of a scam — but never use an email link to sign in, confirm your password or card details, or enter a one-time code. If an email asks for any of that, treat it as phishing and go to Amazon yourself via the app or by typing amazon.co.uk.

**How can I tell who really sent an Amazon email?**

The visible sender name can be faked, so check the full email address and hover over links (without clicking) to see where they really lead. The most reliable test is your account itself: a genuine order or message appears in `Your Orders` and the Message Centre when you sign in directly. If it isn't there, the email is fake.

**I clicked the link and entered my Amazon details — what now?**

Sign in via the app or amazon.co.uk and change your password, then turn on two-step verification and review your orders, saved cards, and addresses. If you entered card details, call your bank using the number on your card and ask them to stop the card and dispute any payment. Then report the email to the NCSC at report@phishing.gov.uk and to Amazon at stop-spoofing@amazon.com.

**Can I get my money back after an Amazon email scam?**

Often, yes. If you paid by card, ask your bank about a chargeback. If you sent a bank transfer, report it as fraud at once: since 7 October 2024, most APP (authorised push-payment) fraud victims can claim reimbursement for UK transfers made over Faster Payments or CHAPS, subject to limits and exclusions — the PSR rules include a 13-month claim window, a maximum claim of £85,000, possible exclusions, and a possible excess of up to £100. Keep every message as evidence and act quickly.

**How do I report a fake Amazon email?**

Forward it to the NCSC at report@phishing.gov.uk and to Amazon at stop-spoofing@amazon.com. If you lost money, also report it to Report Fraud at reportfraud.police.uk or on 0300 123 2040 (England, Wales or Northern Ireland; in Scotland, Police Scotland on 101).
