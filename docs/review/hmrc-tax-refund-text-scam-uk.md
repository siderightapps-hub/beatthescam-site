## 1. What a fake HMRC refund text looks like

An HMRC tax-refund scam text is a smishing message that pretends to be from HM Revenue & Customs so you tap a link and hand over bank, card, or personal details. A typical one reads something like: `HMRC: you are due a tax refund of £274.50. Claim now: hmrc-taxrefund.example`. The promise of 'free money' is the hook, and the amount is usually specific to look official. The link opens a fake GOV.UK-style page that asks for your details to 'process the refund' — and captures them instead. This guide shows how to tell a fake from a genuine HMRC message, how to check safely, and how to report it.


## 2. Why HMRC refund texts are convincing

Scammers spoof the sender name to read 'HMRC' or 'GOV.UK', so a fake can sit alongside any genuine messages — the sender name proves nothing. It's worth knowing that HMRC does run some genuine text and email campaigns, so the common belief that the taxman would never text you isn't quite right and can catch people out. What's reliable is this: HMRC does not ask you to claim a refund, or to hand over your bank or card details, through a link in a text. Genuine tax refunds are issued through your online Personal Tax Account or are repaid automatically — you never have to 'claim' one by following a texted link.


## 3. Signs an HMRC text is a scam

- It offers a tax refund you can 'claim' by tapping a link.
- The link isn't on gov.uk — it's a lookalike, has extra words, or is shortened (the only genuine address ends in gov.uk).
- It asks for your bank details, card number, or personal information to 'release' the refund.
- It creates pressure — a deadline to claim, or a threat of a fine, arrest, or legal action (a common variant).
- It claims a refund is waiting by text; real refunds go through your Personal Tax Account, not a text link.
- Errors can be clues — but a polished, well-written text can still be a scam.


## 4. How the HMRC refund scam works

First, a text claims you're owed a refund or, in the threatening variant, that you owe tax and face action. Second, it pushes you to a link to 'claim' or 'pay'. Third, the link opens a fake GOV.UK-style page asking for your bank or card details and personal information. Fourth, the criminals capture everything you enter. Fifth, they use the bank details to take money, the card details for fraud, and the personal information to impersonate you or target you again. Because the page looks official and the refund feels real, people enter details quickly — so checking only through GOV.UK is what protects you.


## 5. How to check with HMRC safely

Do not tap the link or call a number from the text. To check whether you're owed anything, sign in to your Personal Tax Account by typing gov.uk into your browser yourself and searching for `Personal Tax Account`, or use the official HMRC app. Your refund status and any genuine messages appear there. HMRC won't ask you to claim a refund or share bank details through a texted link, so a message that does is a scam. To contact HMRC, use only the numbers published on GOV.UK. If you're unsure whether a linked site is a copycat of GOV.UK, our guide on /guides/is-this-website-a-scam/ walks through the checks.


## 6. If you clicked or shared details

Act quickly. If you gave bank or card details, contact your bank or card issuer using the number on your card, tell them it was a scam, and ask them to stop the card and watch the account; a card payment may be recoverable through chargeback, and if you made a transfer, report it as fraud — since 7 October 2024, most APP (authorised push-payment) fraud victims can claim reimbursement for UK transfers made over Faster Payments or CHAPS, subject to limits and exclusions — the PSR rules include a 13-month claim window, a maximum claim of £85,000, possible exclusions, and a possible excess of up to £100. If you shared personal information such as your National Insurance number, a Cifas Protective Registration at cifas.org.uk is well worth considering, as it tells lenders to take extra steps to check it's really you; also check your credit report with Experian, Equifax or TransUnion. Government-impersonation texts also target drivers — see our /guides/dvla-vehicle-tax-text-scam/ guide. Keep the text as evidence.


## 7. How to report an HMRC scam text (UK)

Forward the scam text to 60599 — HMRC's number for reporting suspicious texts — and to 7726 so your mobile provider can act. Report scam emails that claim to be from HMRC to phishing@hmrc.gov.uk. If you lost money or shared sensitive details, report it to Report Fraud at reportfraud.police.uk or on 0300 123 2040 if you are in England, Wales or Northern Ireland; in Scotland, report to Police Scotland on 101. Keep a screenshot and a note of what happened.


## FAQ

**Does HMRC send tax refund texts?**

HMRC runs some genuine text and email campaigns, so not every HMRC message is fake — but HMRC does not ask you to claim a refund, or to hand over bank or card details, through a link in a text. Genuine refunds go through your Personal Tax Account on GOV.UK or are repaid automatically. Treat any 'claim your refund here' link as a scam.

**Does HMRC ask for bank details by text?**

No. HMRC won't ask for your bank or card details, or for you to 'confirm' personal information, through a link in a text or email. Check anything about a refund by signing in to your Personal Tax Account on GOV.UK, not through a message.

**I clicked the link and entered my details — what now?**

If you gave bank or card details, call your bank using the number on your card, ask them to stop the card and watch the account, and report any payment. Then report the scam text to 60599 and 7726. If you shared your National Insurance number or other personal details, consider a Cifas Protective Registration at cifas.org.uk to protect your identity.

**I gave my National Insurance number but not bank details — am I at risk?**

Your NI number alone can't be used to empty your account, but it can help criminals impersonate you, so stay alert to unexpected letters, accounts, or credit applications. Consider a Cifas Protective Registration at cifas.org.uk, check your credit report with Experian, Equifax or TransUnion, and report the scam.

**How do I report an HMRC tax refund scam text?**

Forward the text to 60599 (HMRC's reporting number) and to 7726. Report scam emails claiming to be from HMRC to phishing@hmrc.gov.uk. If you lost money or shared details, report it to Report Fraud at reportfraud.police.uk or on 0300 123 2040 (England, Wales or Northern Ireland; in Scotland, Police Scotland on 101).
