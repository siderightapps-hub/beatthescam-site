## 1. What a fake Halifax text looks like

A Halifax scam text — often called smishing, or SMS phishing — is a fake message that pretends to be from the bank so you will tap a link and hand over your login details, card numbers, or one-time passcodes. A typical one reads something like: 'HALIFAX: We've blocked a payment of £79.99 to PAYPAL. If this wasn't you, verify your account here: halifax-secure-verify.com'. The exact wording changes all the time — a new payee added, an account 'locked', a 'suspicious login', or a refund waiting — but the goal never changes: panic you into tapping the link before you think. That link opens a copycat Halifax login page that quietly captures whatever you type. This guide shows you how to tell a genuine Halifax message from a fake, what to do if you have already tapped a link, and how to report it.


## 2. Why a Halifax text can look so convincing

Scammers can set the sender name on a text to display as 'Halifax', which means a fake message can drop into the very same conversation thread as the genuine alerts you have had before. That is why you cannot judge a text by the sender name, or by where it appears in your phone. Criminals also buy UK mobile numbers in bulk and blast the same message to huge numbers of people at once, knowing a slice of them will happen to be Halifax customers. The message is built to get past your judgement, not your bank's security: it borrows Halifax branding, copies the tone of a real fraud alert, and bolts on a deadline so you act in a hurry. None of that makes it real. A genuine Halifax fraud alert will never need you to follow a link to confirm your identity, log in, or move money to a 'safe account'.


## 3. Signs a Halifax text is a scam

- It pressures you to act fast — an account 'locked', a payment 'held', or access lost unless you respond within a deadline.
- It asks you to tap a link to 'verify', 'reconfirm', or 'secure' your account, log in, or re-enter card details.
- The link uses a lookalike or unusual domain — genuine Halifax pages may use halifax.co.uk or Halifax-owned services such as halifax-online.co.uk, but do not use a text-message link to log in, verify details, enter a code, or move money. Open the Halifax app or type halifax.co.uk yourself.
- It asks for your full password, PIN, card number, or a one-time passcode — Halifax will never ask you to share these.
- It tells you to 'protect' your money by moving it to a new or 'safe' account.
- A generic 'Dear Customer' greeting can be a clue, but it is a weaker one by text than by email — genuine bank texts are often short — so weigh it alongside the other signs rather than on its own.
- Small errors can be clues: odd spacing, a foreign number, or phrasing that does not read like an official message — but many scam texts are polished, so a tidy message proves nothing.


## 4. How the Halifax text scam works, step by step

First, you get an unexpected text that appears to come from Halifax, warning of a problem — a blocked payment, a new payee, or a sign-in from an unknown device. Second, it pushes you to tap a link to 'stop' the payment or 'restore' access. Third, the link opens a fake Halifax page that looks almost identical to the real site and asks for your username, password and memorable information. Fourth, the moment you enter them the scammers have your credentials in real time — some fake pages even ask you to 'please wait' while they use the details to log in to your real account. Fifth, they trigger a genuine one-time passcode from Halifax, and the fake page asks you to type it in, which lets them approve a payment or add a new payee. Finally, they move money out, set up new payments, or use your details to apply for credit in your name. The whole sequence can take just a few minutes, which is why slowing down at the very first text matters more than anything else.


## 5. How to check if a Halifax text is genuine (without tapping anything)

Never use a phone number or a link from the text itself — that is exactly how scammers keep you inside their script. Instead you have three safe ways to check. The simplest is to call 159: it is a secure service, recognised across the UK banking industry, that connects you safely and directly to your bank, and Halifax takes part. You can also open the official Halifax app, or type halifax.co.uk into your browser by hand, and check your recent activity and messages there. Or call Halifax on the number printed on the back of your debit or credit card. Not every genuine text will appear as an in-app message, so if you cannot confirm what the text claims, contact Halifax directly rather than assuming either way. When you are not sure whether a linked website is a copycat, our guide on /guides/is-this-website-a-scam/ walks through the checks, and you can paste a suspicious message into our free scam checker for a second opinion.


## 6. What to do if you have already tapped the link or shared details

Act quickly, but do not panic — fast action limits the damage. If you entered your login details or a passcode, call 159 straight away, or use the number on the back of your card, and tell Halifax you think you have been phished; ask them to secure your account and check for payments or new payees you did not set up. Change your Halifax password from inside the official app or by typing the address yourself, and change it anywhere else you reused it. If you shared card details, ask the bank to stop the card and reissue it. If you sent money, tell the bank immediately — since 7 October 2024, most APP (authorised push-payment) fraud victims can claim reimbursement for UK bank transfers made over Faster Payments or CHAPS, subject to limits and exclusions, and there is a deadline for claiming, so report it to your bank as fraud promptly and keep every message. To protect your identity from misuse, consider a Cifas Protective Registration at cifas.org.uk, which tells lenders to take extra steps to check it really is you. You can also review your credit report with Experian, Equifax or TransUnion for any applications you do not recognise. Keep the original text as evidence before you delete it. If your bank is Lloyds, NatWest or another lender, the same steps apply — see, for example, our /guides/lloyds-bank-scam-text/ guide.


## 7. How to report a Halifax scam text in the UK

Reporting takes a minute and helps networks and investigators shut scammers down. Forward the text to 7726 — it is free, and it reports the message to your mobile provider, which can investigate and take action. Report it to Report Fraud at reportfraud.police.uk or on 0300 123 2040 if you are in England, Wales or Northern Ireland; in Scotland, report to Police Scotland on 101. If a similar scam reached you by email, forward it to the National Cyber Security Centre at report@phishing.gov.uk, and you can report suspicious Halifax emails to Halifax at security@halifax.co.uk. Finally, let Halifax know through the app or the number on your card, so they can warn other customers. Keep a screenshot of the message and a note of what you did and when, in case you need it later.


## FAQ

**Is a Halifax text with a link always a scam?**

A link alone is not proof either way. What matters is what the message asks you to do: never use a link in a text to log in, verify your details, enter a one-time code, or move money. If a message pushes you toward any of that, treat it as a scam and check independently — using the Halifax app, the number on the back of your card, or 159.

**Can a scam text appear in my genuine Halifax message thread?**

Yes. Scammers can spoof the sender name so 'Halifax' is displayed, which can place a fake message in the same thread as your real alerts. Never trust a text just because of the sender name or where it sits in your messages — verify it independently before you act on it.

**What number should I call to check a Halifax text safely?**

Call 159 — a secure service that connects you safely and directly to your bank, and Halifax takes part — or use the number printed on the back of your card. Never call a number given in the suspicious text itself.

**I tapped the link and entered my details — what should I do now?**

Call 159 or the number on your card straight away and tell Halifax you have been phished, so they can secure your account. Change your password from inside the official app, ask them to stop your card if you shared card details, and report any payment you did not make. Then report it to Report Fraud at reportfraud.police.uk and consider a Cifas Protective Registration at cifas.org.uk.

**How do I report a fake Halifax text in the UK?**

Forward it to 7726 (free; it reports the message to your mobile provider), then report it to Report Fraud at reportfraud.police.uk or on 0300 123 2040 (England, Wales and Northern Ireland; in Scotland, report to Police Scotland on 101). If it arrived by email, send it to report@phishing.gov.uk or to Halifax at security@halifax.co.uk. Tell Halifax too, through the app or the number on your card.
